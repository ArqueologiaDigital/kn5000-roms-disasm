\chapter{What a block contains}\label{ch:blocks}

Every block opens with a fixed signature, and two of them carry readable names, which let
a program check a stored dump and list what is in it. Beyond that, all three of the \textsc{sound}, \textsc{combination} and
\textsc{sequencer} blocks are laid out below, each to a different depth.

\section{Signatures}\label{sec:signatures}
The bytes below are the first bytes of the reassembled block --- after the payload has been
rejoined from its nibbles, not as they appear on the wire. They were read out of a dump
from a real instrument, and the two rows of each entry are the same sixteen bytes shown
twice, as hexadecimal and as text.

\input{tbl-signatures}

A file whose first bytes do not match the block it claims to be is not a dump of that
category.

The first \textsc{system, part \& midi} block is a signature in the fullest sense: all 32
of its bytes are a constant the instrument copies out of its own program when it
initialises, with one byte filled in afterwards. It carries no settings.

\begin{caution}{The instrument writes its signature but does not check it}
Two routines copy that constant into memory and both are plain copy loops. No comparison
against it was found anywhere in either program image, and none against its leading
\bytes{5A 5A} either. \textbf{Do not expect a block with a wrong signature to be
refused.} Checking it is something a librarian should do; the instrument does not.

The coverage of that negative: every three-byte pointer to the constant, which is two
sites, and every immediate comparison against \bytes{5A 5A} across both images. Code
reaching the constant from a different base, or comparing through a pointer built
elsewhere, would not have been seen.
\end{caution}

\section{Names}
The \textsc{sound} block continues, immediately after its sixteen-byte signature, with
sixteen-byte names in the instrument's own character set, padded with spaces. The second
\textsc{combination} block and the second \textsc{system, part \& midi} block use a
different framing: a two-byte prefix, then a sixteen-byte name, then two further bytes.

A librarian can therefore show the user what a stored file holds --- ``\texttt{SWEEP PAD}'',
``\texttt{Space Infinity}'' --- without decoding anything else.

\section{Inside the SOUND block}\label{sec:soundmemory}
The \textsc{sound} block is sound \emph{memory}, and it is laid out in four banks of
\bytes{10000} bytes. Within a bank the stored sounds are packed back to back, and a
stored sound \textbf{is the same 713-byte layout chapter~\ref{ch:sound} gives for the
sound being edited} --- so its name is its first sixteen bytes, and every parameter in
that chapter is at its stated offset from the start of the record.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
 & at & \\
\midrule
bank \textit{b} & \bytes{10000} $\times$ \textit{b} & four banks \\
stored sounds & \bytes{359} in bank 0, \bytes{090} in the others & 713 bytes each \\
a drum kit's 128 notes & \bytes{B468} & 150 bytes each \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A stored drum kit is likewise the layout of chapter~\ref{ch:sound}: its 128 note records
sit at a 150-byte stride, each beginning with its thirteen-character name.

\section{Inside the COMBINATION block}\label{sec:combmemory}
The larger \textsc{combination} block is \textbf{128 stored combinations of 704 bytes},
arranged as sixteen banks of eight. The banks' names are the last 256 bytes of the smaller
\textsc{combination} block, one name per bank; each combination carries its own
sixteen-character name two bytes into its record.

\subsection*{A combination is a stream of parameter records}
The 704 bytes are not a fixed layout but a \textbf{tagged stream}. Each record is a tag
byte, a length byte, then that many bytes of payload; the stream ends with
\bytes{FF FF} and fills the 704 exactly. Every one of the 128 combinations has the same
23 records in the same order.

\begin{center}
{\small
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
at & tag & length & \\
\midrule
\bytes{000} & \bytes{78} & 16 & the combination's name \\
\bytes{012} & \bytes{60} & 12 & effect common \\
\bytes{020} & \bytes{61} & 30 & \textsc{effect1} \\
\bytes{040} & \bytes{62} & 30 & \textsc{effect2} \\
\bytes{060} & \bytes{63} & 30 & \textsc{reverb} \\
\bytes{080} & \bytes{00}, \bytes{20} & 30 each & part 1, two records \\
\ldots & \ldots & & parts 2 to 8, the same pair \\
\bytes{280} & \bytes{92} & 14 & key scaling and scale tuning \\
\bytes{290} & \bytes{79} & 44 & part common \\
\bytes{2BE} & \bytes{FF FF} & & end of stream \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
}
\end{center}

\begin{note}{The tags are the instrument's own record numbers}
This is what makes the format legible. A tag is not arbitrary: all 23 are record numbers
in the parameter area of chapter~\ref{ch:params}, and a record's payload is that record's
bytes. So \bytes{20} is part~1's own block --- the same \bytes{20} that addresses part~1
in an \textsc{itr} message --- and the parameter tables name its fields directly.

The record numbers also run in address order with exactly two bytes between consecutive
records, which are the tag and length. The stream is a copy of a region of the
instrument's memory with its record headers intact.
\end{note}

\subsection*{One byte the parameter tables do not reach}
A part's first record carries, at \bytes{+15}, six flags that no parameter addresses. Each
says whether the part uses its own value for one of the \textsc{midi multiple messages
output} settings or the instrument's internal one:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
bit & parameter \\
\midrule
0 & \textsc{program change} \\
1 & \textsc{volume} \\
2 & \textsc{panpot} \\
3 & \textsc{chorus depth} \\
4 & \textsc{reverb depth} \\
5 & \textsc{bank select} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Six sibling routines each test one of those bits and then choose between the part's
internal field and its override field; the six parameters are exactly the six the
published tables give an override at all. Bits 6 and 7 are not accounted for.

That also explains the second \textsc{system, part \& midi} block, whose first bytes
chapter~\ref{ch:blocks} lists as \bytes{78 10}: those are not a signature but the first
record's tag and length --- the same stream, beginning with the same name record.

\begin{note}{How this was established, and why it is not a coincidence}
A dump from a real instrument was walked at a 713-byte stride from the first stored
sound. Sixty-four consecutive records began with a printable sixteen-character name, in
every bank, and the drum runs gave 128 consecutive thirteen-character names in every bank.
A stride one byte either side of 713 breaks the run at the first record.

The combination stream is checked differently: its records must tile the 704 bytes and
land on \bytes{FF FF}. All 128 do, with the same 23 records in the same order, and all 23
tags are live record numbers in the parameter area.
\end{note}

\subsection{The tail of a part block}\label{sec:combtail}

\paragraph{Reserved.} Block~A holds the same value in all 3\,080 records at \bytes{+02},
  \bytes{+04}, \bytes{+10} and \bytes{+15}--\bytes{+1A}, and block~B does at
  \bytes{+00}--\bytes{+02}, \bytes{+0D}, \bytes{+11}, \bytes{+12} and \bytes{+15}. Every
  one reads zero. Write zero there.

  \paragraph{A second program and bank.} The last three bytes of each block are a second \textsc{program change \&
  bank} for that part: \bytes{+1B} and \bytes{+1C} are one 7-bit number split four bits
  and three, and \bytes{+1D} is a bank drawn from the same values the part's own bank
  uses. Of the 890 parts carrying a non-zero one, the number and the bank \emph{both}
  match the part's own in 296, and the number never matches without the bank matching too
  --- against a control, the same test run against a neighbouring part, that lands 39
  times. The pair \bytes{+1B},~\bytes{+1C} takes \textbf{exactly 128 distinct values}
  across the corpus, which is what a 7-bit number split four and three must and which no
  rival reading of those two bytes predicts. The shape is confirmed from outside the dump
  as well: the instrument's re-map files are sixteen groups of eight, the same 4+3 split,
  holding entries that are exactly (number, source) pairs. A second copy of an address
  that usually differs from the first is an indirection, which is also why the triple is
  not a function of the program number.

  The program agrees. These bytes are never named by an address --- a part record is
  reached through a pointer, which is why earlier searches for their addresses found
  nothing --- but they are named by displacement off that pointer, and those are short
  distinctive byte strings. There are three sites that write all three bytes and three
  that read all three, in both images, and \textbf{not one site anywhere touches any of
  the three on its own}: they are a single field. At the writing site the values come back
  from a conversion whose input is written to a fixed address as \emph{two} bytes and
  whose result is read from another as \emph{three}, with an inverse beside it that takes
  three and returns two. A three-byte form of a two-byte quantity is exactly what a program
  number and a bank become.

  \paragraph{Three bytes nothing reads.} Block~B's \bytes{+18}--\bytes{+1A} take fifteen distinct combinations
  across the corpus and read \bytes{01 01 01} or \bytes{00 01 01} in four records out of
  five. Nothing names them, and both halves of that have now been measured rather than
  assumed.

  From the data: over all 3\,080 part records and 220 byte columns, none of the three is a
  copy of any other column, the three together are not a copy of any other three-byte run,
  and no column determines any of them --- the best dependency is 0.62 of the target's
  entropy where a shuffled control scores 0.03 and a determination would score 1. The
  closest match, \bytes{+1A} agreeing with \bytes{+03} in 97.6\,\% of records, is two
  nearly-constant columns agreeing and nothing more.

  From the program: a part record is reached through a pointer, so these bytes are named
  by displacement, and there are two displacements they could carry --- block~B sits
  \bytes{20} past block~A. Counting every individual access at either, across all four
  images and not only the two the instrument's panel runs on, there are 127. Three of them
  come near the pointer table that holds part records, and all three are in one routine
  which indexes that table \emph{without} the step that selects a part, so it reaches a
  sibling structure. None reaches a part record. The test for that is controlled: it fires
  on the bytes next door, where 6 of 91 accesses do reach a part record, including the
  sites that write the triple.

  So these three bytes are carried in stored data and copied wholesale, and no code treats
  them as fields. For a librarian that is actionable --- preserve them, do not compute
  them. What remains open is only what they are \emph{for}, and since no code on this
  processor reads them the answer is not in this program; the obvious place to look next is
  the second processor.

  Also open: which side of the indirection the triple holds --- what the part asked for,
  or what it resolved to. Eight of the nine re-maps in the corpus are identity maps, so
  the two sides coincide wherever it could be checked. Deciding it needs a machine with a
  non-identity re-map loaded, dumped.

\section{Inside the SEQUENCER block}\label{sec:seqmemory}
No capture available for this reference contains a \textsc{sequencer} transfer --- the one
to hand came from a rack, which refuses that category. The top-level arrangement is
settled all the same, because the program gives it away, and the record's own contents
are read below from a native sequencer file off a disk, which carries the
\textsc{header} block byte for byte.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
block & size & \\
\midrule
\textsc{location}    & 3\,072 & one song record: the song being edited \\
\textsc{header}      & 30\,720 & \textbf{ten song records of 3\,072} \\
\textsc{performance} & variable & the recorded material \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A song's name is \textbf{six characters at offset \bytes{CA}} of its record. One routine
establishes all of it at once: it blanks that name in three places, and its destinations
are literal --- the \textsc{location} block at \bytes{CA}, and the \textsc{header} block
at $\text{song} \times 3072 + \bytes{CA}$, the multiply written as a shift by eleven plus
a shift by ten. Ten records of 3\,072 fill the block exactly, and the published manual
says the sequencer holds ten songs.

That \textsc{location} carries the name at the \emph{same} offset is what shows it to be a
song record as well, rather than a directory: it is the working copy, in the shape of a
stored one.

\subsection{Inside a song record}

A native sequencer file from a disk, \bytes{01220497.SQF}, is 30\,720 bytes --- the
\textsc{header} block exactly --- and it decodes on the arrangement above without
adjustment: ten records of 3\,072, each opening \bytes{5A 5A 5A 5A}, each with six
printable characters at \bytes{+CA}. Nine of the ten are byte-identical, which is what
ten song slots with one song recorded into them should look like, and the recorded one
differs from the empty template in 727 bytes.

Most of a song record is material this reference already names. From \bytes{+220} it is a
tagged stream in the same form as a combination, \bytes{\{tag, length, payload\}} runs
closed by \bytes{FF FF}, and it falls into three:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
at & records & \\
\midrule
\bytes{+220}--\bytes{+4DF} & 23 & \textbf{a complete combination}, 704 bytes \\
\bytes{+4E0}--\bytes{+AFF} & 49 & parts 8--31, then record \bytes{7A} \\
\bytes{+B00}--\bytes{+B7F} & 5  & records \bytes{98}, \bytes{99}, \bytes{80}, \bytes{91}, \bytes{93} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The first stream is a combination in the strict sense: the same 23 records in the same
order and the same 704 bytes as section~\ref{sec:combmemory} gives, compared field for
field rather than by eye. So a song carries its own combination inside it.

The part records then run \bytes{00}--\bytes{1F} for block~A and \bytes{20}--\bytes{3F}
for block~B, complete and without gaps: \textbf{a song has thirty-two parts}, the same
thirty-two the parameter grammar addresses at block \bytes{20} plus the part number. That
is not only an inference from the tags --- the owner's manual advertises ``32 MIDI parts
available (32-part multi-timbral)'', and in the same breath that the sequencer records
``up to 10 performances'', which is the ten song records this block is made of.
Eight arrive inside the embedded combination and the other twenty-four follow it. Every
one of their fields is named by chapter~\ref{ch:params}, because they are the same
records --- which puts 2\,400 of a song record's 3\,072 bytes under names this reference
already carries.

\paragraph{The 544 bytes ahead of the stream.} Nine of the ten records are identical, so
every byte a song writes is a byte where the recorded one differs, and there are 113 of
them in this region. Read that way it lays out as follows. Fields marked
\emph{unused}~$=$ carry that value in an empty record.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llp{74mm}}
\toprule
at & size & \\
\midrule
\bytes{+000} & 4  & signature \bytes{5A 5A 5A 5A} \\
\bytes{+004} & 1  & \bytes{01} in the recorded song, \bytes{00} in all nine empty ones \\
\bytes{+023} & 16 & a ramp \bytes{01}--\bytes{0F} then \bytes{20} \\
\bytes{+034} & 16 & the same ramp again \\
\bytes{+07E} & 34 & 17 entries of 2 bytes, unused $=$ \bytes{FF FF} \\
\bytes{+0A0} & 17 & 17 entries of 1 byte, unused $=$ \bytes{05} \\
\bytes{+0CA} & 6  & the song \textsc{name} \\
\bytes{+100} & 51 & 17 entries of 3 bytes, unused $=$ \bytes{00 FF FF}; in use the first
                    byte reads \bytes{80} and \textbf{the other two are that track's
                    first block in \textsc{performance}} \\
\bytes{+134} & 204 & 51 entries of 4 bytes, identical in all ten records \\
\bytes{+200} & 7  & a second signature, \bytes{5A 5A 01 00} then \bytes{WA0} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The three tables are the part worth trusting, because they corroborate each other: they
are at different offsets, they mark an unused entry three different ways, and all three
are \textbf{seventeen} entries long. One such count would be an accident of where a reader
chose to stop; three agreeing is a structure. In the recorded song every one of the
seventeen \bytes{+100} entries is in use, and the last ten of their 16-bit values run
consecutively --- which is what a block allocator handing out consecutive blocks looks
like, though this reference does not decode what they index.

The per-track reading is no longer a guess about the \bytes{+100} table: its seventeen
values are the seventeen chains of \textsc{performance} blocks, and following them
produces seventeen \emph{disjoint} chains, which nothing but a real per-track index would
do. The other two tables are the same length and are read the same way here, which is an
inference from their length rather than a measurement.

\textbf{Entry $i$ is track $i$}, and its events address part $i$. A controller event
names the part it addresses in its second data byte, and for sixteen of the seventeen
entries \emph{every} such event in the chain names the entry's own index --- against a
control, each entry paired with the next entry's chain, that matches none of the
seventeen. A chain's last block is only partly used and its tail holds stale bytes, so it
is excluded; with it left in, those stale events show up as the same handful of part
numbers in every chain, which is how the tail was noticed.

The seventeenth is not a recording track, and the evidence is stronger than its not
naming a part. Set its statuses against the other sixteen's and the two sets are
\textbf{disjoint in both directions}. Every one of the sixteen carries notes, controllers
and program changes --- \bytes{90}, \bytes{B0}, \bytes{B4}, \bytes{C0}, \bytes{D1},
\bytes{D2}. Entry~16 carries none of those. It is 999 events of which \textbf{997 are the
clock}, plus exactly two others, whose statuses --- \bytes{80} and \bytes{87} --- appear in
none of the sixteen.

So the seventeen streams are the sixteen recording tracks Technics publishes plus one that
only keeps time. What its two events mean is not established here: the values they carry
appear nowhere in the song header to be checked against.

\paragraph{The 128 bytes after it.} \bytes{+B80} to the end is identical in all ten
records, recorded and empty alike, and is almost entirely zero. Nothing a song does
touches it.

\subsection{PERFORMANCE}

The same disk set carries a companion file of 179\,200 bytes, which is everything the
fixed-size song records are not --- so it is the recorded material, the
\textsc{performance} block. That correspondence comes from how the disk splits the
sequencer and not from anything in the program, and it is the one claim here that rests
on an inference rather than a decode.

\subsubsection*{The area is blocks}

\textsc{performance} is divided into \textbf{256-byte blocks numbered from one}. That is
not inferred: the sequencer turns an address into a block number with three instructions
--- subtract the area's base, shift right by eight, add one --- and turns it back with a
decrement and a shift left by eight.

A block's first five bytes are a header. The \textbf{next block's number is a 16-bit
little-endian value at \bytes{+03}}, which is the offset the same routine reads it from,
and a chain ends on \bytes{0000} or \bytes{FFFF}. The data is \bytes{+05} to \bytes{+FF}.

The seventeen entries in a song's header (section~\ref{sec:seqmemory}) hold each track's
\emph{first} block. Following all seventeen gives 560 blocks and the chains are
\textbf{disjoint} --- no block belongs to two tracks --- which is the first check that the
reading is right. They account for four fifths of the area; the rest is free, and holds
whatever was last written there.

\begin{caution}{A withdrawn hypothesis that was correct, and why the test missed it}
An earlier edition of this chapter recorded, as a lead, that the seventeen values looked
like 256-byte block numbers. It then reported a search for a link field --- four block
sizes, every offset within a block, both byte orders, requiring disjoint chains --- that
found nothing, and withdrew the lead on that basis.

The search was wrong, in one respect: it numbered blocks from zero. Every block it
examined was the one before the block it meant. The hypothesis was right and the test that
appeared to kill it was mis-specified, which is a more dangerous kind of error than a
wrong guess, because a negative result looks like diligence.

What settled it was not a better search. It was reading the four instructions in the
firmware that form a block number, which say \bytes{+1} in as many words.
\end{caution}

\subsubsection*{The events}

Reassembled, a track is a flat stream with no delta before each event: a status byte at or
above \bytes{80}, then a fixed number of data bytes, every one below \bytes{80}. Across
all seventeen tracks that parses \textbf{41\,646 events with not one byte left over}.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
status & data & \\
\midrule
\bytes{81} & 0 & advance the clock one step \\
\bytes{90} & 5 & a note \\
\bytes{B0}, \bytes{B4} & 5 & a controller \\
\bytes{C0} & 5 & a program change \\
\bytes{D2} & 3 & \\
\bytes{E0} & 3 & \\
\bytes{D1} & 2 & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Time is carried by the events themselves. \bytes{81} takes no data and does nothing but
advance the clock, and it is much the commonest status --- 19\,980 of the 41\,646. Every
other event's \textbf{first data byte is its offset inside the current step}: within a
step those offsets never go backwards, 11 times in 15\,306 consecutive pairs, where the
same events shuffled go backwards 38.8\,\% of the time.

A note's five bytes are then the offset, the note number, the velocity, and a gate length
in two 7-bit halves. Over 14\,380 notes the note-number column stays inside 25--126 and
the velocity column inside 1--127, which is what those two columns have to look like and
what no other assignment of the five produces.

A handful of statuses outside the table appear a few times each, at the ends of chains
where a block's last event is cut short; they are not part of the format.

\begin{note}{Reassembly is self-checking}
A block reassembled correctly comes out at exactly the length its own header declared. A
wrong nibble order, or a payload taken with its trailing flag and checksum still attached,
does not. Checking the length is a cheap way to confirm a receiver is unpacking correctly
before trusting anything it produces.
\end{note}
