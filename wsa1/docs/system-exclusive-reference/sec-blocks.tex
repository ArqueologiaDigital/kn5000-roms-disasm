\chapter{What a block contains}\label{ch:blocks}

Every block opens with a fixed signature, and two of them carry readable names, which let
a program check a stored dump and list what is in it. Beyond that, the \textsc{sound} and
\textsc{combination} blocks are laid out below; \textsc{sequencer} is not covered.

\section{Signatures}\label{sec:signatures}
The bytes below are the first bytes of the reassembled block --- after the payload has been
rejoined from its nibbles, not as they appear on the wire. They were read out of a dump
from a real instrument, and the two rows of each entry are the same sixteen bytes shown
twice, as hexadecimal and as text.

A file whose first bytes do not match the block it claims to be is not a dump of that
category.

\section{Names}
The \textsc{sound} block continues, immediately after its sixteen-byte signature, with
sixteen-byte names in the instrument's own character set, padded with spaces. The second
\textsc{combination} block and the second \textsc{system, part \& midi} block use a
different framing: a two-byte prefix, then a sixteen-byte name, then two further bytes.

A librarian can therefore show the user what a stored file holds --- ``\texttt{SWEEP PAD}'',
``\texttt{Space Infinity}'' --- without decoding anything else.

\section{Inside the SOUND block}\label{sec:soundmemory}
The \textsc{sound} block is sound \emph{memory}, and it is laid out in four banks of
\bytes{10000} bytes. Within a bank the stored sounds are packed back to back, and a
stored sound \textbf{is the same 713-byte layout chapter~\ref{ch:sound} gives for the
sound being edited} --- so its name is its first sixteen bytes, and every parameter in
that chapter is at its stated offset from the start of the record.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
 & at & \\
\midrule
bank \textit{b} & \bytes{10000} $\times$ \textit{b} & four banks \\
stored sounds & \bytes{359} in bank 0, \bytes{090} in the others & 713 bytes each \\
a drum kit's 128 notes & \bytes{B468} & 150 bytes each \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A stored drum kit is likewise the layout of chapter~\ref{ch:sound}: its 128 note records
sit at a 150-byte stride, each beginning with its thirteen-character name.

\section{Inside the COMBINATION block}\label{sec:combmemory}
The larger \textsc{combination} block is \textbf{128 stored combinations of 704 bytes},
arranged as sixteen banks of eight. The banks' names are the last 256 bytes of the smaller
\textsc{combination} block, one name per bank; each combination carries its own
sixteen-character name two bytes into its record.

\subsection*{A combination is a stream of parameter records}
The 704 bytes are not a fixed layout but a \textbf{tagged stream}. Each record is a tag
byte, a length byte, then that many bytes of payload; the stream ends with
\bytes{FF FF} and fills the 704 exactly. Every one of the 128 combinations has the same
23 records in the same order.

\begin{center}
{\small
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
at & tag & length & \\
\midrule
\bytes{000} & \bytes{78} & 16 & the combination's name \\
\bytes{012} & \bytes{60} & 12 & effect common \\
\bytes{020} & \bytes{61} & 30 & \textsc{effect1} \\
\bytes{040} & \bytes{62} & 30 & \textsc{effect2} \\
\bytes{060} & \bytes{63} & 30 & \textsc{reverb} \\
\bytes{080} & \bytes{00}, \bytes{20} & 30 each & part 1, two records \\
\ldots & \ldots & & parts 2 to 8, the same pair \\
\bytes{280} & \bytes{92} & 14 & key scaling and scale tuning \\
\bytes{290} & \bytes{79} & 44 & part common \\
\bytes{2BE} & \bytes{FF FF} & & end of stream \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
}
\end{center}

\begin{note}{The tags are the instrument's own record numbers}
This is what makes the format legible. A tag is not arbitrary: all 23 are record numbers
in the parameter area of chapter~\ref{ch:params}, and a record's payload is that record's
bytes. So \bytes{20} is part~1's own block --- the same \bytes{20} that addresses part~1
in an \textsc{itr} message --- and the parameter tables name its fields directly.

The record numbers also run in address order with exactly two bytes between consecutive
records, which are the tag and length. The stream is a copy of a region of the
instrument's memory with its record headers intact.
\end{note}

That also explains the second \textsc{system, part \& midi} block, whose first bytes
chapter~\ref{ch:blocks} lists as \bytes{78 10}: those are not a signature but the first
record's tag and length --- the same stream, beginning with the same name record.

\begin{note}{How this was established, and why it is not a coincidence}
A dump from a real instrument was walked at a 713-byte stride from the first stored
sound. Sixty-four consecutive records began with a printable sixteen-character name, in
every bank, and the drum runs gave 128 consecutive thirteen-character names in every bank.
A stride one byte either side of 713 breaks the run at the first record.

The combination stream is checked differently: its records must tile the 704 bytes and
land on \bytes{FF FF}. All 128 do, with the same 23 records in the same order, and all 23
tags are live record numbers in the parameter area.
\end{note}

\begin{note}{Reassembly is self-checking}
A block reassembled correctly comes out at exactly the length its own header declared. A
wrong nibble order, or a payload taken with its trailing flag and checksum still attached,
does not. Checking the length is a cheap way to confirm a receiver is unpacking correctly
before trusting anything it produces.
\end{note}
