\chapter{Scope of this edition}\label{ch:limits}

This reference covers message framing, the bulk-dump transfer protocol, the parameter
address space, the sound and drum areas, the effects, the universal messages, error
reporting, the differences between the two models, and the layout of the \textsc{sound}
and \textsc{combination} blocks a dump carries.

\section{Deliberately outside it}
\begin{description}
\item[What a parameter does to the sound.] Chapter~\ref{ch:params} enumerates all 108
  addresses of the \bytes{00} area with their name, length, bit mask, accepted values and
  position in a dump, and chapter~\ref{ch:sound} does the same for the sound areas. What a
  setting \emph{does} is the user manuals' subject, not a protocol reference's.
\item[The effect block diagrams.] The guide draws the signal path of each of the 56
  effects. Chapter~\ref{ch:effects} gives their parameters instead.
\end{description}

\section{Open, and what would close it}
\begin{description}
\item[Some bytes inside a combination.] The combination stream is decoded in
  section~\ref{sec:combmemory}, and chapter~\ref{ch:params} names most of what its
  records hold; the effect records are named by chapter~\ref{ch:effects} instead, as a
  type and its values. What was left over has now been read across the whole stored
  corpus --- 385 combinations of eight parts, so 3\,080 part records, from three sources
  produced independently of one another: the 129 presets carried in the program, 128 from
  a user's flash dump, and 128 more from the combination area of a native disk file ---
  and most of it turns out not to be unknown but fixed.

  \emph{Fixed.} Block~A holds the same value in all 3\,080 records at \bytes{+02},
  \bytes{+04}, \bytes{+10} and \bytes{+15}--\bytes{+1A}, and block~B does at
  \bytes{+00}--\bytes{+02}, \bytes{+0D}, \bytes{+11}, \bytes{+12} and \bytes{+15}.
  Every one of them reads zero. Write zero there; a dump that carries anything else is
  something this reference does not describe.

  \emph{Live.} What varies is the last three bytes of each block, and the two blocks
  carry the same shape there: an index \bytes{00}--\bytes{0F}, an index
  \bytes{00}--\bytes{07}, and a flag. They are two fields and not one written twice ---
  they agree in most records and differ in 776. Block~A's is unused by all 1\,024 user
  flash parts and by all but 38 preset parts; block~B's is used in 434 preset parts and
  418 user flash ones. The disk corpus uses neither: all 1\,024 of its parts read zero in
  both, which is the strongest sign yet that the triple is not part of a combination's
  edited state. Ahead of block~B's sit three more,
  \bytes{+18}--\bytes{+1A}, which take fifteen distinct combinations across the corpus
  and read \bytes{01 01 01} or \bytes{00 01 01} in four records out of five.

  The natural reading of that triple is that it caches the sound the part is playing.
  It does not: the same program number carries two different triples in 36 cases, so
  the triple is not determined by the program. The shape it does have --- a sixteen-way
  index, an eight-way index and a flag --- is the shape by which the combination memory
  is itself addressed. Four searches of the program have been run against these bytes
  and all four came back empty or powerless: no menu screen captions them, the setter
  that takes a literal record and offset writes none of them, the six routines that
  reach a part through the instrument's own part pointer are the six that read the
  override byte, and looking for their addresses as literals has no discriminating
  power --- a byte pair occurs by chance often enough in half a megabyte to swamp it,
  as a named byte used as a control demonstrates. They are written through pointers
  built at run time, so naming them needs the writer caught in the act rather than
  found by searching.
\item[Two equaliser addresses.] Chapter~\ref{ch:params} lists \textsc{main out
  equalizer low-freq} and \textsc{high-freq} without a dump position, because the
  descriptors that would place them are the ones whose setter does not build its target
  the way the others do. The stored data now places them anyway, on an argument the
  descriptors cannot make. Record \bytes{79} gives four bytes, \bytes{+01}--\bytes{+04},
  to four parameters. The two \textsc{gain}s are pinned to \bytes{+01} and \bytes{+03} by
  setters read instruction by instruction, so the two frequencies are the two bytes left,
  \bytes{+02} and \bytes{+04}. Three independent corpora agree: 385 combinations hold one
  of two settings, \bytes{D8 02 58 04} or \bytes{D2 01 58 04}, and \textbf{they differ at
  \bytes{+01} and \bytes{+02} only} --- one instrument's low band was moved and its low
  pair moved together, while the high pair did not move at all. The descriptor that would
  put \textsc{low-freq} at \bytes{+01} is contradicted outright: it declares 0--17 and
  \bytes{+01} never reads below 18 in any combination in any corpus.

  What is not settled is the scale. \textsc{high-freq} is declared 17--26 and \bytes{+04}
  reads 4, so either the stored byte is an index into a table rather than the displayed
  value, or the published range is not the stored one. An instrument with both bands moved
  off centre, dumped, would settle it; so would the setter, if it is ever read out.
\item[The rest of a song record, and PERFORMANCE.] Section~\ref{sec:seqmemory} now
  decodes 2\,400 of a song record's 3\,072 bytes, from a native sequencer file off a disk:
  they are the same tagged records a combination is built from, so
  chapter~\ref{ch:params} names their fields. What is left is the 544 bytes ahead of that
  stream, the 128 after it, and the event encoding of \textsc{performance}.
  Section~\ref{sec:seqmemory} now characterises that block from a real file --- an event
  stream, not a record array --- but does not decode its framing, and the six-byte shape
  its opening events suggest is shown there to be an artefact of the first few. A \textsc{sequencer} transfer from an
  SX-WSA1 keyboard would still be the natural way to put that category's frame-level
  behaviour beyond doubt, since the rack refuses it.
\item[Operating system version 1.] Everything here describes version 2.0. Closing this
  needs a dump of the earlier firmware, which this project does not have. This version
  carries no sign of the earlier one: a search of all three program images turns up no
  conversion path and no compatibility text, and nothing compares the version byte a
  block carries. A reader can nonetheless establish what they are holding, two ways.

  Over MIDI: \textsc{ver}, the sixth byte of any message that carries it, is \bytes{11}
  here --- exclusive specification 2.1. A machine speaking a different version announces
  it there, so a receiver can tell at once that it is talking to something this reference
  does not describe.

  At the instrument: it has a \textsc{rom version} screen of its own, which reports its
  ROMs separately as \textsc{wsa-a}, \textsc{wsa-c} and \textsc{wsa-d}.

  And from the front panel, against a document rather than against the machine's own
  account of itself. Technics issued a one-page erratum to the owner's manuals,
  \textsc{qqcg0279a}, whose single change renames the second soft key of the
  \textsc{sound mode} home screen from \textsc{vol} to \textsc{lvl} and makes it
  adjust the level relative to the current value, $-30$ to $+30$. The firmware read
  here already carries the new caption, so it is later than that erratum. The rename
  reached one screen only: the image holds four soft-key rows of that shape, and the
  two whose last key is \textsc{midi} --- the screen the erratum photographs --- read
  \textsc{lvl}, while the two whose last key is \textsc{part} still read
  \textsc{vol}. An instrument whose \textsc{sound mode} home screen says \textsc{vol}
  is older than the machine this reference describes.
\end{description}
