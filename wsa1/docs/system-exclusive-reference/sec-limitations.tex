\chapter{Scope of this edition}\label{ch:limits}

This reference covers message framing, the bulk-dump transfer protocol, the parameter
address space, the sound and drum areas, the effects, the universal messages, error
reporting, the differences between the two models, and the layout of the \textsc{sound}
and \textsc{combination} blocks a dump carries.

\section{Deliberately outside it}
\begin{description}
\item[What a parameter does to the sound.] Chapter~\ref{ch:params} enumerates all 108
  addresses of the \bytes{00} area with their name, length, bit mask, accepted values and
  position in a dump, and chapter~\ref{ch:sound} does the same for the sound areas. What a
  setting \emph{does} is the user manuals' subject, not a protocol reference's.
\item[The effect block diagrams.] The guide draws the signal path of each of the 56
  effects. Chapter~\ref{ch:effects} gives their parameters instead.
\end{description}

\section{Open, and what would close it}
\begin{description}
\item[Three bytes inside a combination.] The combination stream is decoded in
  section~\ref{sec:combmemory}, and chapter~\ref{ch:params} names most of what its
  records hold; the effect records are named by chapter~\ref{ch:effects} instead. What was
  left over has been read across the whole stored corpus --- 385 combinations of eight
  parts, so 3\,080 part records, from three sources produced independently of one another:
  the 129 presets carried in the program, 128 from a user's flash dump, and 128 more from
  the combination area of a native disk file.

  \emph{Reserved.} Block~A holds the same value in all 3\,080 records at \bytes{+02},
  \bytes{+04}, \bytes{+10} and \bytes{+15}--\bytes{+1A}, and block~B does at
  \bytes{+00}--\bytes{+02}, \bytes{+0D}, \bytes{+11}, \bytes{+12} and \bytes{+15}. Every
  one reads zero. Write zero there.

  \emph{Named.} The last three bytes of each block are a second \textsc{program change \&
  bank} for that part: \bytes{+1B} and \bytes{+1C} are one 7-bit number split four bits
  and three, and \bytes{+1D} is a bank drawn from the same values the part's own bank
  uses. Of the 890 parts carrying a non-zero one, the number and the bank \emph{both}
  match the part's own in 296, and the number never matches without the bank matching too
  --- against a control, the same test run against a neighbouring part, that lands 39
  times. The pair \bytes{+1B},~\bytes{+1C} takes \textbf{exactly 128 distinct values}
  across the corpus, which is what a 7-bit number split four and three must and which no
  rival reading of those two bytes predicts. The shape is confirmed from outside the dump
  as well: the instrument's re-map files are sixteen groups of eight, the same 4+3 split,
  holding entries that are exactly (number, source) pairs. A second copy of an address
  that usually differs from the first is an indirection, which is also why the triple is
  not a function of the program number.

  The program agrees. These bytes are never named by an address --- a part record is
  reached through a pointer, which is why earlier searches for their addresses found
  nothing --- but they are named by displacement off that pointer, and those are short
  distinctive byte strings. There are three sites that write all three bytes and three
  that read all three, in both images, and \textbf{not one site anywhere touches any of
  the three on its own}: they are a single field. At the writing site the values come back
  from a conversion whose input is written to a fixed address as \emph{two} bytes and
  whose result is read from another as \emph{three}, with an inverse beside it that takes
  three and returns two. A three-byte form of a two-byte quantity is exactly what a program
  number and a bank become.

  \emph{Inert.} Block~B's \bytes{+18}--\bytes{+1A} take fifteen distinct combinations
  across the corpus and read \bytes{01 01 01} or \bytes{00 01 01} in four records out of
  five. Nothing names them, and both halves of that have now been measured rather than
  assumed.

  From the data: over all 3\,080 part records and 220 byte columns, none of the three is a
  copy of any other column, the three together are not a copy of any other three-byte run,
  and no column determines any of them --- the best dependency is 0.62 of the target's
  entropy where a shuffled control scores 0.03 and a determination would score 1. The
  closest match, \bytes{+1A} agreeing with \bytes{+03} in 97.6\,\% of records, is two
  nearly-constant columns agreeing and nothing more.

  From the program: a part record is reached through a pointer, so these bytes are named
  by displacement, and there are two displacements they could carry --- block~B sits
  \bytes{20} past block~A. At the first, no group of instructions touches all three, in
  either image. At the second there are five, and not one of them reaches a part record;
  two are inside a 64-entry uniform table that the reference disassembler refuses to
  decode as instructions at all. The test for ``does this reach a part record'' is the
  step that turns a table index into a part slot, and it is controlled: it fires on the
  sites that write the triple next door, and on none of these five.

  So these three bytes are carried in stored data and copied wholesale, and no code treats
  them as fields. For a librarian that is actionable --- preserve them, do not compute
  them --- which makes it a closed question rather than an open one, even though the bytes
  still have no name.

  Also open: which side of the indirection the triple holds --- what the part asked for,
  or what it resolved to. Eight of the nine re-maps in the corpus are identity maps, so
  the two sides coincide wherever it could be checked. Deciding it needs a machine with a
  non-identity re-map loaded, dumped.
\item[Which track is which.] Section~\ref{sec:seqmemory} covers a song record's shape
  entirely --- 2\,400 of its 3\,072 bytes are the same tagged records a combination is
  built from, the 544-byte header is laid out field by field, and the 128-byte tail is
  untouched by any song --- and \textsc{performance} is decoded: 256-byte blocks numbered
  from one, chained by a 16-bit link at \bytes{+03}, carrying a flat event stream whose
  statuses and lengths are tabulated there.

  What is left is a labelling question rather than a structural one. The song header holds
  three seventeen-entry tables and Technics publishes sixteen recording tracks, so there is
  one entry more than there are tracks. Which entry belongs to which track, and what the
  seventeenth carries, would be settled by one recording made on a known single track.
\item[Operating system version 1.] Everything here describes version 2.0. Closing this
  needs a dump of the earlier firmware, and there is not one to be had here: the archive
  this project works from carries a single firmware set, whose own accompanying note calls
  version 2 ``probably the last released version''. This is an absence of an artefact
  rather than a piece of analysis left undone. This version
  carries no sign of the earlier one: a search of all three program images turns up no
  conversion path and no compatibility text, and nothing compares the version byte a
  block carries. A reader can nonetheless establish what they are holding, two ways.

  Over MIDI: \textsc{ver}, the sixth byte of any message that carries it, is \bytes{11}
  here --- exclusive specification 2.1. A machine speaking a different version announces
  it there, so a receiver can tell at once that it is talking to something this reference
  does not describe.

  At the instrument: it has a \textsc{rom version} screen of its own, which reports its
  ROMs separately as \textsc{wsa-a}, \textsc{wsa-c} and \textsc{wsa-d}.

  And from the front panel, against a document rather than against the machine's own
  account of itself. Technics issued a one-page erratum to the owner's manuals,
  \textsc{qqcg0279a}, whose single change renames the second soft key of the
  \textsc{sound mode} home screen from \textsc{vol} to \textsc{lvl} and makes it
  adjust the level relative to the current value, $-30$ to $+30$. The firmware read
  here already carries the new caption, so it is later than that erratum. The rename
  reached one screen only: the image holds four soft-key rows of that shape, and the
  two whose last key is \textsc{midi} --- the screen the erratum photographs --- read
  \textsc{lvl}, while the two whose last key is \textsc{part} still read
  \textsc{vol}. An instrument whose \textsc{sound mode} home screen says \textsc{vol}
  is older than the machine this reference describes.
\end{description}
