\chapter{The sound areas}\label{ch:sound}

\textsc{normal sound} and \textsc{drum sound} are the two areas of the address map that
hold an instrument sound rather than a setting. They are reached the same way as any other
parameter, with \textsc{itr} to write and \textsc{drq} to request.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textsc{adr} & address & area \\
\midrule
\bytes{10 00 00} & \bytes{040000} & \textsc{normal sound}, individual \\
\bytes{18 00 00} & \bytes{060000} & \textsc{drum sound}, individual \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{caution}{This is the sound being edited, not a stored one}
These addresses reach the instrument's working copy --- the sound currently loaded for
editing. They are not a way to read or write one of the 32 stored sounds. For those, use
the \textsc{sound} bulk-dump category, which carries the whole sound memory at
\bytes{20 00 00}.
\end{caution}

\section{How a normal sound is laid out}
The \textsc{normal sound} area is \textbf{713 bytes}. It is one \textsc{general data}
block, then four identical \textsc{tone data} blocks, then four identical
\textsc{modeling data} blocks --- the instrument's four tones and the four driver and
resonator models that go with them.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrl}
\toprule
block & size & repeats & parameter numbers \\
\midrule
\textsc{general data}  & 217 & 1 & \bytes{000}--\bytes{0D8} \\
\textsc{tone data}     &  81 & 4 & \bytes{0D9}, \bytes{12A}, \bytes{17B}, \bytes{1CC} \\
\textsc{modeling data} &  43 & 4 & \bytes{21D}, \bytes{248}, \bytes{273}, \bytes{29E} \\
\midrule
total & 713 & & \bytes{000}--\bytes{2C8} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A parameter number is an offset from the start of the area, so the address of parameter
\textit{p} of tone \textit{n} (counting tones from 1) is

\begin{center}
\bytes{040000} $+$ \bytes{0D9} $+$ 81 $\times$ (\textit{n} $-$ 1) $+$ \textit{p}
\end{center}

expressed as \textsc{adr} in the usual three seven-bit bytes, and the same with \bytes{21D}
and 43 for a modeling block. The tables below give one instance of each repeating block;
the other three are identical at the offsets above.

\begin{note}{Two independent sources agree on the size}
The block sizes and repeat counts here come from Technics' published parameter tables. The
figure of 713 bytes for the whole area comes from the program, decoded separately, and the
guide never states it. The transcribed blocks tile \bytes{000} to \bytes{2C8} with no gap
and no overlap and come to exactly that total, which is what makes the layout above
trustworthy rather than merely plausible.
\end{note}

\section{How a drum sound is laid out}
The \textsc{drum sound} area is \textbf{19\,608 bytes} and is arranged quite differently:
a kit, then a table choosing a sound for each of the 128 note numbers, then 128 note
records --- one per key.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrl}
\toprule
block & size & repeats & parameter numbers \\
\midrule
\textsc{kit data}     & 152 &   1 & \bytes{000}--\bytes{097} \\
\textsc{sound select} & 256 &   1 & \bytes{098}--\bytes{197}, two bytes per note \\
\textsc{note data}    & 150 & 128 & \bytes{198} $+$ 150 $\times$ note \\
\midrule
total & 19\,608 & & \bytes{000}--\bytes{4C97} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Each note record is itself three kinds of block, and a drum note has \emph{two} tones and
two modeling blocks where a normal sound has four:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrl}
\toprule
block & size & repeats & offset within the note \\
\midrule
\textsc{note general data}  &  18 & 1 & \bytes{000} \\
\textsc{note tone data}     &  23 & 2 & \bytes{012}, \bytes{029} \\
\textsc{note modeling data} &  43 & 2 & \bytes{040}, \bytes{06B} \\
\midrule
total & 150 & & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The modeling block is the same 43-byte layout a normal sound uses. The tone block is not:
a drum tone has no pitch or filter envelope of its own and no layer ranges, which is what
takes it from 81 bytes down to 23.

\begin{note}{The note positions are checked against the guide's own table}
The guide prints the starting number of every one of the 128 notes. The layout above
implies them instead --- \bytes{198} plus 150 per note --- and the two agree wherever they
were compared, including at the last row, \textsc{note:7f}, which both put at
\bytes{4C02}. With the area also totalling the 19\,608 bytes the program gives, that is
three independent routes to the same arithmetic.
\end{note}

\section{What is transcribed here, and how far it is checked}
The tables give each parameter's number, its bit field within that byte, its name and its
range. They were read from scans of the published tables, and the numbering is checked by
the tiling described above. \textbf{The names and bit fields are not checked by
anything}; a misreading of one would survive.

A \emph{range} is checked wherever the guide gives it twice, once in hex and once in
decimal, which it does on 12 rows. Eleven agree. The twelfth is a misprint in the guide,
and is the one place in these tables where the printed page and what is set here differ:

\begin{caution}{Two misprints in the guide's drum tables}
\textsc{drum sound / note tone data / pitch key shift}, \bytes{1AE}, is printed with the
span \bytes{E8\textasciitilde 18} beside the note \textsc{-\,50\textasciitilde+\,50}.
\bytes{E8}--\bytes{18} read as a signed byte is $-24$ to $+24$, and the identical row in
\textsc{normal sound tone data}, \bytes{0DD}, carries the same span beside
\textsc{-\,24\textasciitilde+\,24}. The hex is right; the table above prints $-24$ to
$+24$. The \textsc{50} appears to have come from the neighbouring signed rows on the same
page, every one of which is \bytes{CE\textasciitilde 32}.

On the same page the \textsc{2nd} column of \textsc{note tone data} is misnumbered. A
23-byte block starting at \bytes{012} puts its second instance at \bytes{029}, and the
column's tail (\bytes{030} onward) agrees --- but its first seven entries read
\bytes{024} to \bytes{02A}, five too low, and the column jumps from \bytes{02A} to
\bytes{030}. The numbering above is derived from the stride, so it is unaffected; a
reader taking the second tone's addresses off the printed column would write seven
parameters to the wrong bytes.
\end{caution}

The enumerations these tables point at --- the control functions a controller can be
assigned to, the filter \textsc{value} bytes for each filter mode, and the
\textsc{interaction connect} table --- are in chapter~\ref{ch:enums}.
